# Market and Evidence Brief

Last reviewed: 2026-08-15. Statistics are labeled by source-data year so older
surveillance is not presented as a current 2026 measurement.

## Population and need

The potential user population is large, but disability is not one homogeneous market.
The product should segment by functional constraints and available movements rather
than diagnosis.

The U.S. figures are prevalence context, not a serviceable-market calculation. They do
not establish willingness to use or pay for this product. A credible commercial model
needs interviews, prototype testing, acquisition-cost experiments, and retention data.

## Health disparities

CDC's 2022 disability infographic reports the following age-adjusted comparisons:

| Condition | Adults with disabilities | Adults without disabilities |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Obesity | 40.5% | 30.3% |
| Diabetes | 16.6% | 7.9% |
| Heart disease | 10.4% | 3.7% |

Source: [CDC, Disability Impacts All of Us](https://www.cdc.gov/disability-and-health/media/pdfs/disability-impacts-all-of-us-infographic.pdf).

WHO reports that persons with disabilities have approximately twice the risk of
conditions including depression, asthma, diabetes, stroke, obesity, and poor oral
health. WHO also identifies physical inactivity as one of several elevated
noncommunicable-disease risk factors. These are population-level associations shaped
by access, environment, discrimination, income, and health-system barriers; they do
not mean disability itself directly causes each condition.

CDC's 2009–2012 analysis found one or more selected chronic diseases among 40.5% of
working-age adults with disabilities, compared with 13.7% of adults without
disabilities. The same study found 47.1% versus 26.1% were aerobically inactive.

No broad disability-to-blood-clotting statistic is included. Clot risk varies greatly
by immobility, surgery, injury, medication, age, and specific condition; presenting a
single disability-wide correlation would be medically misleading. This product must
not assess or claim to reduce clot risk.

## Evidence that activity can help

The U.S. Physical Activity Guidelines state that physical activity reduces the risk of
heart disease, stroke, high blood pressure, type 2 diabetes, depression, and several
cancers, while improving physical function and quality of life. Adults with
disabilities who cannot meet the standard target should remain active according to
their abilities and avoid inactivity. HHS also notes that even five minutes of
activity has health benefits and can be built up over time. Sources: [HHS guidelines
summary](https://odphp.health.gov/our-work/nutrition-physical-activity/physical-activity-guidelines/current-guidelines/top-10-things-know) and [HHS disability activity guidance](https://odphp.health.gov/myhealthfinder/health-conditions/obesity/stay-active-disability-quick-tips).

Disability-specific evidence supports the product's adaptation model while also
showing why claims must stay modest:

- A 2025 systematic review of 46 disability exercise studies identified individualized
  intensity, exercise modifications or alternatives, accommodations, and adherence
  support as recurring implementation needs. The studies were heterogeneous, so the
  authors did not pool clinical effects. [Exercise Considerations for Adults with
  Disabilities](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10882-025-10016-w)
- A 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis found exercise programs were associated
  with reduced depressive symptoms among people with disabilities, but disability
  types and interventions varied. [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37372720/)
- A 2024 meta-analysis of 19 randomized studies involving 797 adults with spinal cord
  injury found improvements in well-being and health-related quality of life, with
  low-certainty evidence and four serious intervention-attributable adverse events.
  This reinforces the need for stop guidance and conservative progression.
  [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38556188/)

The product can therefore say it helps users discover and perform general-wellness
exercise around their stated abilities. It cannot say its recommendation or pose model
diagnoses, treats, rehabilitates, prevents complications, or produces the same outcomes
as supervised clinical care.

## Competitive landscape

The market contains both broad fitness platforms and narrower adaptive products:

- Apple Watch supports wheelchair-specific activity goals and outdoor wheelchair
  walking/running workout modes, demonstrating mainstream demand for mobility-aware
  tracking. [Apple mobility accessibility](https://www.apple.com/by/accessibility/mobility/)
- Wheel With Me Adapt Fit and Wheel Fit focus on seated or wheelchair-user workouts.
  [Wheel With Me](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/wheel-with-me-adapt-fit/id1609250295) and
  [Wheel Fit](https://www.wheelfit.app/)
- Exercise Buddy focuses on structured visual exercise instruction, assessment, and
  progress monitoring for autistic people and diverse learners.
  [Exercise Buddy](https://exercisebuddy.com/exercise-buddy-app/)
- AdaptiveFit combines workout content with adaptive-trainer services for a broad set
  of physical, cognitive, and mental adaptive needs.
  [AdaptiveFit](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/adaptivefit/id6505124309)
- AdaptiveLift and movement-health products personalize around pain, injury history,
  or movement assessment, but are not necessarily disability-first.
  [AdaptiveLift](https://www.adaptivelift.app/) and [movr](https://www.movewithmovr.com/)

The defensible product gap is not simply “fitness for disabled people.” Existing
products already occupy that description. The sharper position is:

> Constraint-first exercise discovery and workout generation that explains every
> compatibility decision, handles equipment as optional or substitutable, and accepts
> privacy-preserving form metrics without storing camera footage.

## Initial market segment

The practical beachhead is adults who:

- Have stable movement constraints, limb differences, mobility limitations, or
  temporary/chronic knee or back limitations.
- Want non-clinical home or gym exercise rather than rehabilitation treatment.
- Can state which regions/capabilities they prefer, limit, or avoid.
- Need seated, supported, unilateral, low-impact, or equipment-substituted options.
- Are comfortable using a phone or web app and can stop or seek professional guidance
  when symptoms warrant it.

This deliberately excludes acute injury, unexplained severe pain, immediate post-op
care, and users needing clinician-supervised rehabilitation.

## Validation plan

Before treating the concept as product-market fit:

1. Interview at least 15–20 people across wheelchair users, ambulatory disabled users,
   limb-difference users, and people with recurring knee/back limitations.
2. Include lived-experience reviewers in taxonomy, safety-copy, and exercise-content
   decisions; do not rely only on clinicians or nondisabled developers.
3. Test whether users can correctly express constraints without diagnosis-based forms.
4. Measure compatible-workout completion, exercise swaps, warning acknowledgements,
   abandoned sessions, and reasons for rejection.
5. Compare camera feedback enabled versus disabled; the core discovery product must
   remain useful without camera permission or tracking support.
6. Have a qualified adaptive fitness professional review every active exercise,
   modification, warning, and tracking target before a public health claim or launch.

Primary validation metrics should be successful compatible-workout creation,
completion rate, repeat-week activity, false-incompatibility reports, unsafe-option
reports, and user trust in explanations—not calories burned or medical outcomes.
